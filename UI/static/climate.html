How the significance test works

  1. 1

    Fit a line

    Least squares finds the straight line that sits closest to all of the annual values.

  2. 2

    Measure the wobble

    The gaps between each year and the line estimate normal year-to-year variation. We adjust for correlation between neighbouring years using Newey–West standard errors.

  3. 3

    Compare slope with noise

    The t statistic divides the slope by its standard error. A large absolute value means the trend is large compared with the year-to-year scatter.

  4. 4

    Convert to a p-value

    The p-value is the chance of seeing a slope at least this steep if the true trend were zero. A value below 0.05 is the conventional threshold for statistical significance.

What “statistically significant” means — and does not mean

A p-value is not the probability that the trend is real, and it says nothing about how large or important the change is. It only measures how compatible this record is with a null model of no trend. A significant result means the signal is hard to explain by the observed year-to-year noise; a non-significant result means this record cannot separate the trend from that noise.

R² is the share of year-to-year variation explained by the straight line, the typical scatter is the standard deviation of annual values around it, and the 95% confidence interval is the range of slopes compatible with the record at the conventional 95% level.

Data: ERA5 reanalysis via the Open-Meteo Archive API. One grid cell per region. Annual temperature is a mean of daily means; annual rainfall is a sum of daily precipitation.
